## 1. Telehealth vs in-person physio for knee osteoarthritis (PEAK trial)

- Full citation: Hinman RS, Campbell PK, Kimp AJ, Russell T, Foster NE, Kasza J, Harris A, Bennell KL. "Telerehabilitation consultations with a physiotherapist for chronic knee pain versus in-person consultations in Australia: the PEAK non-inferiority randomised controlled trial." The Lancet, 2024, volume 403, pages 1267-1278. DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(23)02630-2. PMID 38461844. Source: Europe PMC record https://europepmc.org/article/MED/38461844 (abstract read via the Europe PMC API, checked 2026-10-01). Canonical DOI link: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(23)02630-2 (publisher page blocks automated access; the DOI is confirmed from the Europe PMC record).
- Sample size: 394 adults enrolled, 204 in-person and 190 telerehabilitation (video); 383 (97%) gave 3-month primary outcome data; recruited from 27 Australian clinics. Source: PEAK abstract, Europe PMC https://europepmc.org/article/MED/38461844 - "394 adults were enrolled" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Design: both groups had five consultations over 3 months covering strengthening, physical activity and education; primary outcomes were knee pain (0-10) and function (WOMAC 0-68) at 3 months. Source: same - "five consultations over 3 months" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Conclusion: video physiotherapy was non-inferior to in-person care for pain (mean difference 0.16, 95% CI -0.26 to 0.57) and function (1.65, 95% CI -0.23 to 3.53). Source: same - "Telerehabilitation with a physiotherapist is non-inferior to in-person care" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Safety: adverse events were similar between groups (40 [21%] in-person, 35 [19%] telerehabilitation) and none were serious. Source: same - "none were serious" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Scope limits for honest wording: the trial was in adults with chronic knee pain consistent with osteoarthritis, in Australia, with in-person physiotherapists delivering video care. Do not generalise beyond knee osteoarthritis.
- NOT VERIFIED - do not use: the brief's "Annals of Internal Medicine" idea. The PEAK primary paper is in The Lancet. Do not state superiority claims (for physical activity, satisfaction etc.): they were reported in secondary commentary I did not verify on a primary page.

## 2. Systematic review on telerehabilitation for musculoskeletal conditions

- Citation: Cottrell MA, Galea OA, O'Leary SP, Hill AJ, Russell TG. "Real-time telerehabilitation for the treatment of musculoskeletal conditions is effective and comparable to standard practice: a systematic review and meta-analysis." Clinical Rehabilitation, 2017, 31(5), 625-638. DOI 10.1177/0269215516645148. Source: Europe PMC https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27141087 (abstract read via the Europe PMC API; canonical DOI link https://doi.org/10.1177/0269215516645148) (checked 2026-10-01)
- Size: 13 studies (n = 1520) met the criteria; six databases searched to November 2015. Source: same - "13 studies (n = 1520)" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Conclusion: real-time telerehabilitation appears effective and comparable to conventional delivery for physical function and pain across a variety of musculoskeletal conditions. Source: same - "effective and comparable to conventional methods of healthcare delivery" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Detail: treatment given solely by telerehabilitation was equivalent to face-to-face for physical function (SMD 0.14, 95% CI -0.10 to 0.37); pain improvement was comparable between groups. Source: same (checked 2026-10-01)
- Caveat for wording: studies were few and varied (I-squared up to 96% for some pooled results). Say "appears comparable", never "proves".

## 3. CSP and NHS England guidance on remote consultations

- CSP supports a hybrid, personalised, flexible mix of remote and in-person physiotherapy. Source: CSP, Remote consultations - CSP guidance for your practice https://www.csp.org.uk/professional-clinical/professional-guidance/remote-consultations/csp-guidance - "hybrid, personalised, flexible blend of remote and in-person physiotherapy delivery" (checked 2026-10-01)
- CSP says in-person is the better option for some people in some situations. Source: CSP news, Mix of in-person and remote consultations best https://www.csp.org.uk/news/2022-02-09-mix-person-remote-consultations-best-csp - "for some people, in some situations, in-person services will be the best option" (checked 2026-10-01; quote read via page summary, wording is close to the page, paraphrase rather than quote it)
- CSP says safe remote services need urgent referral pathways for in-person assessment or investigation. Source: CSP, How to ensure remote consultation services are safe https://www.csp.org.uk/professional-clinical/professional-guidance/remote-consultations/csp-guidance-2 - "Urgent referral pathways for in-person assessment and/or investigation" (checked 2026-10-01)
- CSP says safe services need good triage and a safety risk assessment with escalation plans. Source: same page - "High quality referral and triage processes" (checked 2026-10-01)
- NHS England guide (suitability): remote MSK physiotherapy suits people who do not need an in-person physical examination or who have less complex problems. Source: NHS England, Guide to adopting remote consultations in adult MSK physiotherapy services https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/guide-to-adopting-remote-consultations-in-adult-musculoskeletal-physiotherapy-services/ - "suitable for people who do not require an in-person physical examination" (checked 2026-10-01)
- NHS England guide (diagnostic uncertainty): where diagnostics may be needed, suitability of remote care should be carefully considered; remote examination may lead to imaging being over requested, especially for complex or uncertain cases. Source: same - "suitability of remote consultation should be carefully considered" (checked 2026-10-01)
- NOT VERIFIED - do not use: any CSP list of exact conditions or red flags that force a switch to face to face. The CSP pages I could read give principles (personalised, safe, urgent in-person pathways), not a condition list. Use the NHS England guide and the NHS red-flag pages (items 9 and 10) for "when to see someone in person".

## 4. NHS Scotland MSK waiting times (Public Health Scotland)

- Published 23 June 2026. Between August 2025 (when the new guidance took full effect for completed waits) and March 2026, on average 52.4% of patients waited four weeks or less to be seen. Source: Public Health Scotland, AHP musculoskeletal waiting times in NHS Scotland, data to 31 March 2026 https://www.publichealthscotland.scot/publications/allied-health-professionals-musculoskeletal-waiting-times-in-nhs-scotland/allied-health-professionals-musculoskeletal-waiting-times-in-nhs-scotland-quarterly-and-monthly-data-to-31-march-2026/ - "on average 52.4% of patients waited four weeks or less" (checked 2026-10-01)
- 75,128 patients were waiting to be seen at 31 March 2026. Source: same - "there were 75,128 patients waiting to be seen" (checked 2026-10-01)
- The Scottish Government target is that at least 90% of patients wait no longer than four weeks. Source: same (checked 2026-10-01; wording on the page: "at least 90% of patients should wait no longer than four weeks")
- Careful: the 52.4% figure is for COMPLETED waits (patients already seen). A different figure, 36.5%, is for people still waiting (average July 2025 to March 2026). Do not mix them up, and always say "seen within four weeks" for 52.4%. These are all allied health professional MSK services, not physiotherapy alone.

## 5. Self-referral to NHS MSK physiotherapy in Scotland

- Access depends on the local health board: each board decides whether you can self-refer or need a referral from a GP or other professional. Source: NHS inform, How to access MSK services https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/muscle-bone-and-joints/how-to-access-msk-services/ - "Your local health board decide how each service is accessed" (checked 2026-10-01; page last updated 28 July 2026)
- Same page lists physiotherapy among common MSK treatments and links each Scottish health board. Source: same (checked 2026-10-01)
- NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde allows adults to self-refer to its MSK physiotherapy service if eligible (registered with a local GP, and not in excluded groups such as recent surgery or pregnancy). Source: NHSGGC, Self referral to adult MSK physiotherapy https://www.nhsggc.scot/hospitals-services/services-a-to-z/musculoskeletal-msk-physiotherapy/self-referral-to-adult-msk-physiotherapy/ (checked 2026-10-01; read via page summary - say "adults can usually self-refer if they meet the criteria" and link the page, do not list exclusions)
- NHS England-wide pages (sciatica, tennis elbow etc.) say: "In many areas you may be able to get help, such as physiotherapy, from NHS community musculoskeletal (MSK) services without needing a referral from a GP." Source: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sciatica/ (checked 2026-10-01). That wording is for the NHS in general; use the NHS inform page for Scotland.

## 6. "Physiotherapist" is a protected title; HCPC registration

- Physiotherapist is a protected title; anyone using it must be on the HCPC Register, and misuse can lead to prosecution. Source: HCPC, Misuse of title https://www.hcpc-uk.org/concerns/what-we-investigate/misuse-of-title/ - "anyone who uses a designated title must be on our Register" (checked 2026-10-01)
- The public can check a registrant on the HCPC Register (register search). Source: HCPC site; the search tool URL was not separately fetched. NOT VERIFIED - do not use a specific register-search URL unless re-checked.

## 9. NICE and NHS pages per condition

### Low back pain and sciatica (NICE NG59)

- NG59 "Low back pain and sciatica in over 16s: assessment and management", published 30 November 2016, last updated 29 July 2026. Source: NICE https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/Recommendations (checked 2026-10-01)
- Exercise: consider a group exercise programme within the NHS for people with an episode or flare-up of low back pain with or without sciatica. Source: same, rec 1.2.2 - "Consider a group exercise programme" (checked 2026-10-01). Do not say NICE says exercise is the only first-line treatment; say exercise is among recommended non-invasive options.
- Self-management and normal activity: NICE recommends encouraging continued normal activities. Source: same, section 1.2 - "encouragement to continue with normal activities" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Imaging: do not routinely offer imaging in a non-specialist setting for low back pain with or without sciatica. Source: same, rec 1.1.4 - "Do not routinely offer imaging in a non-specialist setting" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Manual therapy is only recommended as part of a package that includes exercise. Source: same, section 1.2 - "only as part of a treatment package that includes exercise" (checked 2026-10-01; recommendation amended 2026)
- Do not offer acupuncture, ultrasound or spinal injections for managing low back pain. Source: same, recs 1.2.8, 1.2.9, 1.3.1 (checked 2026-10-01)
- NHS back pain: stays active, improves on its own within a few weeks. Source: NHS, Back pain https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/ - "Back pain often improves on its own within a few weeks" (checked 2026-10-01)
- NHS sciatica: usually improves in weeks to months; carry on normal activities; physiotherapy is among treatments. Source: NHS, Sciatica https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sciatica/ - "usually gets better in a few weeks to a few months" (checked 2026-10-01; first quote read via page summary, close to page wording)

### Knee pain and knee osteoarthritis (NICE NG226)

- NG226 "Osteoarthritis in over 16s: diagnosis and management", published 19 October 2022. Source: NICE https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226/chapter/Recommendations (checked 2026-10-01)
- Core treatments are therapeutic exercise and weight management (if appropriate), with information and support. Source: same, rec 1.2.2 - "the core treatments for the condition are therapeutic exercise" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Do not offer acupuncture or dry needling for osteoarthritis. Source: same, rec 1.3.8 (checked 2026-10-01)
- Do not offer glucosamine or strong opioids for osteoarthritis. Source: same, rec 1.4.6 (checked 2026-10-01)
- NHS osteoarthritis: aim for 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly plus strength exercises; more severe symptoms may need a structured exercise plan with a physiotherapist. Source: NHS, Osteoarthritis https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/osteoarthritis/ - "structured exercise plan with a physiotherapist" (checked 2026-10-01)
- NHS knee pain: can often be treated at home; call 111 if the pain is very bad. Source: NHS, Knee pain https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/knee-pain/ - "Call 111 if the pain is very bad" (checked 2026-10-01)

### Neck pain

- Most neck pain lasts a few weeks; try neck flexibility exercises; do not wear a neck collar; see a GP if it does not go away after a few weeks or if there are pins and needles or a cold arm. Source: NHS, Neck pain https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/neck-pain-and-stiff-neck/ - "Most neck pain only lasts a few weeks" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Physiotherapy from NHS community MSK services may be available without a GP referral in many areas. Source: same - "without needing a referral from a GP" (checked 2026-10-01)

### Shoulder pain (rotator cuff / frozen shoulder)

- Try shoulder exercises for 6 to 8 weeks to stop pain returning; physiotherapy is an option; urgent GP/111 help for sudden or very bad shoulder pain. Source: NHS, Shoulder pain https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/shoulder-pain/ - "try shoulder exercises for 6 to 8 weeks" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Frozen shoulder: painful and stiff for months, sometimes years; gentle exercises at home; physiotherapy can help get movement back; physiotherapy usually lasts at least 6 weeks. Source: NHS, Frozen shoulder https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/frozen-shoulder/ - "Physiotherapy can help you get movement back" (checked 2026-10-01)
- NOT VERIFIED - do not use: any NICE guidance page specific to rotator cuff or frozen shoulder (I did not locate one). Use NHS pages only.

### Plantar fasciitis (heel pain)

- You can usually ease it yourself; see a GP if no improvement within 2 weeks; try regular gentle exercises to stretch the sole of the foot and heel; a physiotherapist can show exercises. Source: NHS, Plantar fasciitis https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/plantar-fasciitis/ - "see a GP if it does not improve within 2 weeks" (checked 2026-10-01)

### Tennis elbow

- Usually goes away with rest but can sometimes last over a year; physiotherapy may help if symptoms have not improved after 6 weeks of home treatment; treatments may include stretching and strengthening for wrist and forearm. Source: NHS, Tennis elbow https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tennis-elbow/ - "Physiotherapy may help if your symptoms have not improved" (checked 2026-10-01)

### Hip pain / hip osteoarthritis / gluteal tendinopathy

- Try gentle hip stretching exercises; physiotherapy for more help with exercises; urgent GP/111 help if severe hip pain starts suddenly without a fall or injury, or the hip is swollen and hot. Source: NHS, Hip pain in adults https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/hip-pain/ - "physiotherapy for more help with exercises" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Hip osteoarthritis: see NICE NG226 above (covers osteoarthritis generally) and NHS Osteoarthritis page.
- NOT VERIFIED - do not use: any NHS or NICE page specific to gluteal tendinopathy. The NHS hip pain page mentions tendinopathy only briefly. Do not write a gluteal tendinopathy treatment claim.

## 10. Cauda equina red flags (NHS)

- No standalone nhs.uk cauda equina page was found (the obvious URL returns not found). The NHS back pain and sciatica pages carry the red-flag lists below.
- Back pain, call 999 or go to A&E if you have back pain and: pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs; a loss of feeling around your genitals or anus; changes in bladder or bowels such as difficulty peeing or peeing or pooing yourself; changes in sexual feeling or function; chest pain; or it started after a serious accident. Source: NHS, Back pain https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/ - "a loss of feeling around your genitals or anus" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Sciatica, go to A&E or call 999 if you: have sciatica on both sides; have severe or worsening weakness or numbness in both legs; have numbness around or under your genitals or around your bottom; find it hard to start peeing, cannot pee or cannot control when you pee; or cannot control or do not notice when you poo, and this is not normal for you. Source: NHS, Sciatica https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sciatica/ - "have sciatica on both sides" (checked 2026-10-01; list read from page text)
- NHS adds that these could be symptoms of a serious back problem needing hospital treatment as soon as possible, and not to drive to A&E (ask someone to drive you or call 999). Source: NHS, Sciatica https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sciatica/ - "Do not drive to A&E" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Content rule: because PhysioOnClick is online only, say clearly that these symptoms need emergency in-person care and a video appointment is not the right route.
