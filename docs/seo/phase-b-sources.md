## 1. Telehealth vs in-person physio for knee osteoarthritis (PEAK trial)

- Full citation: Hinman RS, Campbell PK, Kimp AJ, Russell T, Foster NE, Kasza J, Harris A, Bennell KL. "Telerehabilitation consultations with a physiotherapist for chronic knee pain versus in-person consultations in Australia: the PEAK non-inferiority randomised controlled trial." The Lancet, 2024, volume 403, pages 1267-1278. DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(23)02630-2. PMID 38461844. Source: Europe PMC record https://europepmc.org/article/MED/38461844 (abstract read via the Europe PMC API, checked 2026-10-01). Canonical DOI link: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(23)02630-2 (publisher page blocks automated access; the DOI is confirmed from the Europe PMC record).
- Sample size: 394 adults enrolled, 204 in-person and 190 telerehabilitation (video); 383 (97%) gave 3-month primary outcome data; recruited from 27 Australian clinics. Source: PEAK abstract, Europe PMC https://europepmc.org/article/MED/38461844 - "394 adults were enrolled" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Design: both groups had five consultations over 3 months covering strengthening, physical activity and education; primary outcomes were knee pain (0-10) and function (WOMAC 0-68) at 3 months. Source: same - "five consultations over 3 months" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Conclusion: video physiotherapy was non-inferior to in-person care for pain (mean difference 0.16, 95% CI -0.26 to 0.57) and function (1.65, 95% CI -0.23 to 3.53). Source: same - "Telerehabilitation with a physiotherapist is non-inferior to in-person care" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Safety: adverse events were similar between groups (40 [21%] in-person, 35 [19%] telerehabilitation) and none were serious. Source: same - "none were serious" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Scope limits for honest wording: the trial was in adults with chronic knee pain consistent with osteoarthritis, in Australia (27 clinics, 15 primary care physiotherapists; randomisation stratified by physiotherapist and clinic). Do not say who delivered each arm: the abstract does not state it. Do not generalise beyond knee osteoarthritis.
- Not verified: the "Annals of Internal Medicine" idea. The PEAK primary paper is in The Lancet. Do not state superiority claims (physical activity, satisfaction etc.): they were not confirmed on a primary page. NOT VERIFIED - do not use.

## 2. Systematic review on telerehabilitation for musculoskeletal conditions

- Citation: Cottrell MA, Galea OA, O'Leary SP, Hill AJ, Russell TG. "Real-time telerehabilitation for the treatment of musculoskeletal conditions is effective and comparable to standard practice: a systematic review and meta-analysis." Clinical Rehabilitation, 2017, 31(5), 625-638. DOI 10.1177/0269215516645148. Source: Europe PMC https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27141087 (abstract read via the Europe PMC API; canonical DOI link https://doi.org/10.1177/0269215516645148) (checked 2026-10-01)
- Size: 13 studies (n = 1520) met the criteria; six databases searched to November 2015. Source: same - "13 studies (n = 1520)" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Conclusion: real-time telerehabilitation appears effective and comparable to conventional delivery for physical function and pain across a variety of musculoskeletal conditions. Source: same - "effective and comparable to conventional methods of healthcare delivery" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Detail: treatment given solely by telerehabilitation was equivalent to face-to-face for physical function (SMD 0.14, 95% CI -0.10 to 0.37); pain improvement was comparable between groups. Source: same (checked 2026-10-01)
- Caveat for wording: studies were few and varied (I-squared up to 96% for some pooled results). Say "appears comparable", never "proves".

## 3. CSP and NHS England guidance on remote consultations

- CSP supports a hybrid, personalised, flexible mix of remote and in-person physiotherapy. Source: CSP, Remote consultations - CSP guidance for your practice https://www.csp.org.uk/professional-clinical/professional-guidance/remote-consultations/csp-guidance - "hybrid, personalised, flexible blend of remote and in-person physiotherapy delivery" (checked 2026-10-01)
- CSP says in-person is the better option for some people in some situations. Source: CSP news, Mix of in-person and remote consultations best https://www.csp.org.uk/news/2022-02-09-mix-person-remote-consultations-best-csp - "for some people, in some situations, in-person services will be the best option" (checked 2026-10-01; PARAPHRASE ONLY - quote not re-confirmed)
- CSP says safe remote services need urgent referral pathways for in-person assessment or investigation. Source: CSP, How to ensure remote consultation services are safe https://www.csp.org.uk/professional-clinical/professional-guidance/remote-consultations/csp-guidance-2 - "Urgent referral pathways for in-person assessment and/or investigation" (checked 2026-10-01)
- CSP says safe services need good triage and a safety risk assessment with escalation plans. Source: same page - "High quality referral and triage processes" (checked 2026-10-01)
- NHS England guide (suitability): remote MSK physiotherapy suits people who do not need an in-person physical examination or who have less complex problems. Source: NHS England, Guide to adopting remote consultations in adult MSK physiotherapy services https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/guide-to-adopting-remote-consultations-in-adult-musculoskeletal-physiotherapy-services/ - "suitable for people who do not require an in-person physical examination" (checked 2026-10-01)
- NHS England guide (diagnostic uncertainty): where diagnostics may be needed, suitability of remote care should be carefully considered; remote examination may lead to imaging being over requested, especially for complex or uncertain cases. Source: same - "suitability of remote consultation should be carefully considered" (checked 2026-10-01)
- NOT VERIFIED - do not use: any CSP list of exact conditions or red flags that force a switch to face to face. ## 6. "Physiotherapist" is a protected title; HCPC registration

- Designated titles are protected by law and anyone using one must be on the HCPC Register; an unregistered person may be committing an offence even if they only describe their service as "physiotherapy". Source: HCPC, Misuse of title https://www.hcpc-uk.org/concerns/what-we-investigate/misuse-of-title/ - "anyone who uses a designated title must be on our Register" (checked 2026-10-01). Note: this page does not itself list the titles; it names "physiotherapy" only as an example of a service description.
- "Physiotherapist" and "Physical therapist" are the protected titles for physiotherapists. Source: HCPC, Professions and protected titles https://www.hcpc-uk.org/about-us/who-we-regulate/the-professions/ - "Protected titles Physiotherapist Physical therapist" (checked 2026-10-01 via direct read of page text)
- The public can check a registrant on the HCPC Register (register search). Source: HCPC page above mentions "Check the Register"; the search tool URL was not separately fetched. Not verified: a specific register-search URL; do not use one unless re-checked.

## 11. Urgent and emergency routing (NHS pages, read 2026-10-01)

Rule: never route someone less urgently than the NHS page does. Each page's own route is recorded below. Page text was read via a page-to-text fetch tool, so check any later quote again before relying on exact punctuation.

- Heart attack: call 999 for chest pain that feels tight or squeezing, and for chest pain spreading to the arms, neck or jaw; also severe breathing difficulty. Do not drive yourself. Source: NHS, Heart attack https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heart-attack/ - "chest pain that's spreading to your arms, neck or jaw" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Stroke: FAST = face, arms, speech, time to call 999; other signs include weakness or numbness down one side, blurred vision or loss of sight, dizziness. Call 999 now; do not drive yourself to A&E. Source: NHS, Stroke symptoms https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/stroke/symptoms/ - "Call 999 now if you think you're having" (checked 2026-10-01; PARAPHRASE ONLY for the rest). The main page https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/stroke/ says a stroke "needs urgent medical help in hospital".
- Neck pain: the page read has only a GP route (see a GP if it does not go away after a few weeks, or with pins and needles or a cold arm). No 999 or 111 section was found on it. Source: https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/neck-pain-and-stiff-neck/ (checked 2026-10-01). So neck red flags such as dizziness, visual symptoms, or weakness or numbness in both arms are not on that page; the stroke page (999) covers arm weakness or numbness, blurred vision and dizziness, so route those to 999.
- Dislocated shoulder: go to A&E; call 999 if you cannot get there yourself; do not drive to A&E. Signs listed include shoulder looks out of place or changed shape, cannot move the arm, painful, bruised or swollen. Source: NHS, Dislocated shoulder https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dislocated-shoulder/ - "Call 999 for an ambulance if you cannot get to A&E" (checked 2026-10-01)
- Shoulder pain: urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 (not 999) for sudden or very bad pain, cannot move the arm, changed shape or badly swollen, pins and needles that do not go away, no feeling in the arm, hot or cold arm, pain after an injury such as a fall, severe pain in both shoulders, feeling feverish or unwell. Source: https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/shoulder-pain/ - "Ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111" (checked 2026-10-01). Chest pain with shoulder pain is covered by the heart attack entry (999).
- Knee pain: NHS 111 (or an urgent treatment centre) if the knee is very painful, you cannot move it or put weight on it, it is badly swollen or changed shape, it locks, gives way or painfully clicks, or you have a high temperature with redness or heat around the knee. No 999 or A&E section found. Source: https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/knee-pain/ - "your knee locks, gives way or painfully clicks" (checked 2026-10-01). See a GP if it does not improve within a few weeks.
- Plantar fasciitis (for Task 7): GP, not urgent: severe pain or stopping normal activities, pain not improving after 2 weeks of self-care, tingling or loss of feeling, diabetes with foot pain. No 999/111 route found. Source: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/plantar-fasciitis/ (checked 2026-10-01)

## 13. Post-surgery recovery

- Knee replacement: it may take several months or longer to fully recover; recovery from a partial replacement should be shorter than from a total replacement. Home exercise programme is explained by a physiotherapist or occupational therapist before discharge; following the exercises early helps long-term strength and movement. Source: NHS, Recovering from a knee replacement https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/knee-replacement/recovery/ - "It may take several months or longer to fully recover" (checked 2026-10-02; page last reviewed 9 September 2026)
- Knee replacement timings (same page): usually home 1 to 4 days after the operation; try walking without an aid after about 6 weeks if ready; wait at least 6 weeks to drive after a total knee replacement or 3 weeks after a partial one, and check with the doctor; return to work usually after about 6 to 12 weeks depending on the work; avoid heavy household lifting for the first 3 months; follow-up appointment about 6 weeks after the operation. Quote: "return to work when you feel ready" (checked 2026-10-02; PARAPHRASE ONLY for the timings). The page also says to follow the physiotherapist's exercises "as these will help your recovery and prevent complications".
- Knee replacement complications and routing: Source: NHS, Complications of a knee replacement https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/knee-replacement/complications/ (checked 2026-10-02). Urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 if recently had a knee replacement and: throbbing or cramping pain in the leg; a high temperature or feeling hot, cold or shivery; oozing or pus from the wound; redness, tenderness, swelling or pain in the knee that is not getting better or is getting worse (page says these could be signs of an infection or a blood clot). Call 999 or go to A&E if pain and swelling in the leg AND difficulty breathing or chest pains (possible pulmonary embolism); do not drive to A&E. Quote: "Call 999 or go to A&E if" (PARAPHRASE ONLY for the rest). The page also says physiotherapy and certain exercises may help with problems bending the knee, ongoing pain and stiffness, or instability.
- Hip replacement: may take several months to recover; physiotherapist or occupational therapist explains home exercises before discharge and early exercise helps long-term strength and movement; usually home about 1 to 3 days after if generally fit and surgery went well; follow-up around 6 to 12 weeks; wait at least 6 weeks to drive (check with doctor); return to work usually about 6 weeks depending on work; do not bend the hip more than 90 degrees, do not cross legs, avoid jumping or sudden turns. Source: NHS, Recovering from a hip replacement https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/hip-replacement/recovering-from-a-hip-replacement/ - "follow the exercises your physiotherapist has recommended" (checked 2026-10-02; page last reviewed 6 March 2024; PARAPHRASE ONLY for the timings).
